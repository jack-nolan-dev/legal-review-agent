# Legal Knowledge Base — Small Business Reference

This is a reference guide for common legal areas that affect small businesses in the United States. It is not exhaustive and is not a substitute for legal advice. Use it to ground your analysis and identify relevant legal domains. Always verify that statutes cited here are current before relying on them.

---

## 1. Wiretapping & Call Recording

**The rule:** US states split into one-party consent and all-party (two-party) consent for recording calls.

**One-party consent (federal default + most states):** Under 18 U.S.C. §2511, you can record a call you're a party to without telling the other person. Most states follow this.

**All-party consent states (as of 2024):** California, Connecticut, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Vermont, Washington. Some of these are criminal statutes (MA, FL, IL, PA), not just civil.

**Key cases:**
- *Commonwealth v. Hyde*, 434 Mass. 610 (2001) — MA Supreme Court upheld criminal conviction for secretly recording police officers. Confirms MA's strict all-party rule.
- *Kearney v. Salomon Smith Barney*, 39 Cal. 4th 95 (2006) — CA law applies when one party is in CA, even if the other is in a one-party state.

**Common gotcha:** Interstate calls — if either party is in an all-party state, treat it as all-party. The stricter state's law generally controls.

**Compliant path:** Always disclose recording at the start of the call and get verbal consent. "This call may be recorded for quality purposes — is that okay?" Wait for a "yes."

## 2. Cold Email — CAN-SPAM Act

**The rule:** The CAN-SPAM Act (15 U.S.C. §7701 et seq.) governs all commercial email messages. There is no meaningful B2B exemption.

**Requirements (every commercial email):**
1. Accurate "From" header and subject line
2. Identify the message as an advertisement (if applicable)
3. Include a valid **physical postal address** — PO box or registered commercial mail agent is fine
4. Working **unsubscribe mechanism** — must be processed within 10 business days
5. Honor opt-outs promptly; never sell or transfer opted-out addresses

**Penalties:** Up to $51,744 per email (adjusted for inflation). FTC enforces.

**Common gotcha:** The physical address requirement catches many startups. A virtual mailbox service (e.g., iPostal1, Earth Class Mail) satisfies this — you don't need a real office.

**State laws:** California (CalOPPA), Virginia, Colorado, and Connecticut have additional email/privacy requirements. Check state law if sending volume is significant.

## 3. Cold Calls — TCPA & Do-Not-Call

**The rule:** The Telephone Consumer Protection Act (47 U.S.C. §227) and the FTC Telemarketing Sales Rule (16 CFR Part 310) regulate cold calls.

**Key requirements:**
1. Check the **National Do-Not-Call Registry** before calling any consumer number
2. Do not call before 8 AM or after 9 PM (recipient's local time)
3. Identify yourself and your business at the start of the call
4. No autodialers or prerecorded messages to cell phones without prior express consent
5. Maintain an internal do-not-call list; honor requests immediately

**B2B exception:** The Do-Not-Call Registry applies to residential numbers only. Business-to-business calls to a business line are generally exempt. But: if you call a small business owner's personal cell phone, it's a consumer number.

**Penalties:** $500–$1,500 per violation. Private right of action.

**State laws:** Many states have their own telemarketing statutes. Some (e.g., Florida under §501.059) are stricter than federal law.

## 4. Auto-Renewing Subscriptions — ROSCA

**The rule:** The Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act (15 U.S.C. §8401 et seq.) requires:
1. Clear disclosure of the terms **before** obtaining billing info
2. Explicit informed consent (affirmative action, not pre-checked boxes)
3. Simple cancellation mechanism

**State laws:**
- **California Auto-Renewal Law (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §17600 et seq.):** Requires clear terms, affirmative consent, acknowledgment email, and easy online cancellation. Very actively enforced.
- **Massachusetts (940 CMR 38.00):** Pre-charge disclosure, renewal reminders, easy cancel.
- **New York (GBL §527-a):** Similar disclosure and cancellation requirements.

**Common gotcha:** "Dark patterns" that make it hard to cancel (hiding the cancel button, requiring a phone call) violate these laws. The FTC's 2024 "click-to-cancel" rule requires cancellation to be as easy as signup.

**Compliant path:** Show terms clearly, get a checkbox (not pre-checked), send a confirmation email with terms, and put a cancel button in the account dashboard.

## 5. Copyright & Fair Use

**The rule:** Copyright (17 U.S.C. §101 et seq.) protects original creative works from the moment of creation. You need a license or fair use defense to use someone else's work.

**What's protected:** Photos, text, code, music, videos, website designs, graphics, logos (also trademark — see §6).

**Fair use (17 U.S.C. §107):** A defense, not a right. Four factors:
1. Purpose and character (commercial use weighs against; transformative weighs for)
2. Nature of the original work
3. Amount used relative to the whole
4. Effect on the market for the original

**Common small-business violations:**
- Scraping photos from Google Images, Yelp, or a competitor's website
- Using stock photos without the proper license tier (e.g., web-only license used in print)
- Screenshots of other websites used in marketing materials
- Copying website copy or blog posts

**Penalties:** $750–$30,000 per work infringed (statutory damages); up to $150,000 if willful. Plus attorney's fees.

**Compliant path:** Use licensed stock photos (Unsplash, Pexels for free; Shutterstock, Adobe Stock for paid). Get written permission for any third-party content. When building demo/mockup websites, use placeholder images — never scrape a real business's photos.

## 6. Trademark

**The rule:** Trademark law (Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. §1051 et seq.) protects brand identifiers — names, logos, slogans — from use that creates a likelihood of confusion.

**Key concepts:**
- Using another business's name in plaintext to describe them is generally fine (nominative fair use)
- Using their **stylized logo** without permission is risky — it implies endorsement/affiliation
- Creating a website that looks like it's officially from another business = infringement + potentially unfair competition

**Common gotcha for agencies/freelancers:** Building a demo website for a prospective client using their logo, brand colors, and photos before they've hired you. If publicly accessible, this creates confusion about affiliation.

**Compliant path:** Use the business name in plain text only. No stylized logos. Include a visible "unofficial concept — not affiliated with or endorsed by [Business Name]" disclaimer. Password-protect demos so they're not publicly crawlable.

## 7. Right of Publicity

**The rule:** The right of publicity protects a person's name, image, and likeness from commercial use without consent. This is state law — about 35 states recognize it.

**Key states:**
- **California (Cal. Civ. Code §3344):** Statutory right; damages + profits + punitive damages.
- **New York (N.Y. Civ. Rights Law §50–51):** Statutory right; criminal penalties possible.
- **Indiana, Tennessee, Texas:** Broad statutes, including post-mortem rights.

**Common small-business violations:**
- Using real customers' names or photos in testimonials without written consent
- Scraping Google/Yelp reviews and attributing them to named individuals on your website
- Using a local celebrity's or influencer's likeness in marketing

**Compliant path:** Get signed release/consent forms before using anyone's name, photo, or testimonial commercially. For reviews: link to the original platform or use anonymized quotes ("A Google reviewer said...").

## 8. State Consumer Protection — Unfair & Deceptive Practices

**The rule:** Every US state has a consumer protection statute (often called "little FTC acts"). These prohibit unfair or deceptive business practices, and many allow private lawsuits with enhanced damages.

**Key states:**
- **Massachusetts Ch. 93A:** Triple damages + mandatory attorney's fees. Very plaintiff-friendly. Covers B2B disputes too. Any misleading statement in commerce can trigger liability.
- **California UCL (Bus. & Prof. Code §17200):** Prohibits unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business practices. Broad standing.
- **New York GBL §349:** Deceptive acts or practices. $50 statutory damages minimum, treble up to $1,000.
- **Florida FDUTPA (§501.204):** Actual damages or declaratory/injunctive relief.

**Common gotcha:** Overpromising in sales pitches. Saying "we built your website" about an unsolicited demo they never asked for = potentially deceptive. Saying "free no-obligation concept" = accurate.

**Compliant path:** Be truthful in all marketing and sales communications. Disclose material terms. Don't create false impressions of affiliation, endorsement, or existing business relationships.

## 9. Business Entity & Liability

**The rule:** How you structure your business determines personal liability exposure.

**Sole proprietorship / general partnership:** Default for one person or two+ people in business together. No liability protection — each owner is personally liable for all business debts and the other partner's business-related actions.

**LLC:** Limited liability company. Shields personal assets from business liabilities (with exceptions for fraud, personal guarantees, and failure to maintain corporate formalities). Relatively cheap and simple to form.

**Key considerations:**
- Two people doing business together without an agreement = general partnership by default = unlimited mutual liability
- A single breach-of-contract claim or IP lawsuit can exceed business revenue
- LLC formation costs are modest ($500–$2,000 including filing fees + operating agreement)

**Compliant path:** If your business has any meaningful liability exposure (client work, contracts, handling data), form an LLC. Get an operating agreement. Maintain separate business banking.

## 10. Contracts — Key Clauses for Service Businesses

**Essential clauses for client service agreements:**

1. **Scope of work:** What you're delivering, what you're not
2. **Payment terms:** Amount, schedule, late fees, kill fee for cancellation
3. **Limitation of liability:** Cap liability at fees paid under the contract
4. **Warranty disclaimer:** No guarantee of specific business results (e.g., "increased sales")
5. **Client content indemnification:** Client warrants they own/have rights to content they provide
6. **IP transfer on payment:** Work product transfers to client only after full payment
7. **Termination clause:** How either party can exit, with what notice and what happens to work in progress
8. **Dispute resolution:** Mediation/arbitration before litigation; governing law and venue

**Common gotcha:** Handshake deals or email-only agreements. Without a signed contract, you have no limitation of liability and disputes become he-said-she-said.

**Compliant path:** Use a written contract for every engagement, even small ones. Have a lawyer review your template once — then reuse it.

## 11. Data Privacy

**The rule:** No single federal privacy law (for most businesses), but a growing patchwork of state laws.

**Key regulations:**
- **CCPA/CPRA (California):** Applies if you collect personal info from CA residents AND meet revenue/data thresholds ($25M+ revenue, 100K+ consumers' data, or 50%+ revenue from selling data). Most small businesses are exempt but should still follow best practices.
- **Virginia CDPA, Colorado CPA, Connecticut CTDPA, Utah UCPA:** Similar frameworks with varying thresholds.
- **COPPA (15 U.S.C. §6501):** If your service is directed at children under 13, strict consent and data handling requirements apply.
- **HIPAA:** If you handle protected health information (healthcare businesses, certain apps).
- **GDPR:** If you serve EU residents (even from the US).

**Compliant path for small businesses:**
1. Have a privacy policy that accurately describes what you collect and why
2. Don't collect more data than you need
3. Use HTTPS
4. Don't sell or share personal data without disclosure
5. Have a data breach response plan (even informal)
6. If you handle payment data, follow PCI-DSS basics

## 12. Employment & Contractor Classification

**The rule:** Misclassifying employees as independent contractors is a major enforcement priority for the IRS and DOL.

**IRS test (simplified):** The more control you have over *how* the work is done (not just *what*), the more likely the worker is an employee.

**DOL "economic reality" test (updated 2024):** Six factors including opportunity for profit/loss, degree of control, permanence of relationship, skill required, and whether work is integral to the business.

**State tests:**
- **California AB 5 / ABC test:** Worker is an employee unless (A) free from control, (B) doing work outside the usual course of business, AND (C) independently established in that trade. Very strict.
- **Massachusetts (M.G.L. c. 149 §148B):** Similarly strict ABC test.

**Common gotcha:** Hiring a "freelancer" who works only for you, on your schedule, using your tools = employee, regardless of what the contract says.

**Penalties:** Back taxes, penalties, back wages, benefits, plus potential state fines.

**Compliant path:** If someone works regular hours, uses your tools, and you control how they do their work — they're an employee. If they're truly independent (multiple clients, own tools, control their own schedule), document the relationship clearly in writing.
